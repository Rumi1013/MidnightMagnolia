The Five Roots

A Midnight Magnolia Theory of Change — not a program to complete, but a living practice to return to. Built from the inside out: from lived experience, from the traditions that held when nothing else could, from the frameworks that gave language to what was already known.

The full framework document — with each pillar expanded, all source attributions, the Layered Access Model, and the Brand Language Guide — lives in Midnight_Magnolia_Theory_of_Change.docx. This tab is the living reference summary.

Core Premise

Healing is not a destination. It is a living practice, approached through a spiral rather than a staircase. Your survival was not a mistake. Your healing is not a performance. Your worth is not conditional on your productivity. You come from people. Those people are still with you.

The Ancestral Ground
The Ancestral Ground
"Before the roots, there is the soil."

This is not a pillar — it is the foundation all five roots grow from. Hoodoo, mediumship, and ancestor veneration rooted in Black Southern Lowcountry tradition. Named here as context, not curriculum. It is the epistemological ground beneath the framework: a way of knowing that is lineage-based, place-based, and body-based. The dead remain in relationship with the living. Recovery is always intergenerational.

Spiritual ground · Hoodoo · Mediumship · Ancestor veneration · Lowcountry, SC — present as context, not available as curriculum
The Five Pillars
01
Name It
"You cannot heal what you cannot name. Emotional literacy is not a luxury — it is the first act of care."

Most people were taught to manage feelings, not name them. Name It interrupts that training. It asks: what is actually happening in this body, right now? The Feelings Wheel moves from surface word to root word — from "I feel bad" to "I feel ashamed, and underneath that, I feel afraid." CBT maps the thought-feeling-behavior loop. Mediumship teaches the more subtle skill: distinguishing what is yours from what is being transmitted through you.

Feelings Wheel · CBT · Mediumship attunement
02
Burn It
"Release shame without burning the self. We stop turning pain into a verdict."

Shame is not feedback. It is what happens when pain meets judgment instead of witness. Burn It creates a container for releasing the stories that punish survival. MARA holds the code: non-judgment. Recovery Dharma holds the First Noble Truth: there is suffering — not as verdict, but as fact that makes practice possible. Hoodoo has always included literal fire — candle work, petition burning, cleansing smoke. To practice hoodoo openly is itself an act of burning stigma; Black spiritual traditions were criminalized and pathologized. Reclamation is sacred act.

Burn the Stigma · MARA · Recovery Dharma (First Noble Truth) · Hoodoo fire practice
03
Examine It
"Honest inventory is not self-indictment. It is curiosity with enough love to look clearly."

After shame is released, the question becomes: what remains? The Four Agreements function as an examination toolkit: be impeccable with your word — first to yourself. Don't make assumptions — especially about your own unworthiness. SMART Recovery's Cost-Benefit Analysis asks honestly: what is this costing me? Ancestor veneration requires looking honestly at lineage — the gifts and the wounds both. The genealogical research is itself the most rigorous self-inventory there is: sitting with what was passed down, what was broken, what survived, what is yours to change.

The Four Agreements · SMART Recovery (CBA) · CBT restructuring · Ancestor veneration · Lineage work
04
Tend It
"Cultivation over correction. You are not a problem to be fixed. You are a life to be tended."

Correction assumes something is wrong. Cultivation assumes something is alive. Recovery Dharma's Eightfold Path offers a daily ethic — wise effort, wise intention, wise livelihood — not commandments but orientation. SMART's Lifestyle Balance Wheel reveals where you're depleting without receiving. The Fourth Agreement: always do your best — which changes from day to day, energy to energy. An ancestor altar is tended with regularity, not urgency. This is what cultivation looks like in spiritual form: not crisis prayer, but ongoing loving attention. Samuel Vincent continued in relationship through exactly this kind of sustained attention.

Recovery Dharma (Eightfold Path) · SMART Recovery (Balanced Living) · 4 Agreements · Altar practice
05
Root It
"Connection is not extra. It is the ground everything else grows from."

The wounds that bring most people to this framework were relational in origin — and relational healing is part of their repair. AA's First Tradition: our common welfare should come first. MARA: our recovery may depend upon our unity. Recovery Dharma places sangha at the center of the practice — not as support structure for the real work, but as part of the real work. Ancestor veneration is community extending across time. The Lowcountry roots — the Gullah Geechee spiritual tradition, the African diasporic thread that hoodoo carries — understand community as medicine not merely in metaphor but in practice. You do not belong only to living people. You belong to a lineage.

The Vincent Line — Six Generations
Generation 1 · ~1820
Greene Vincent
Born: February 1820 · Person County, NC · Died: unknown, North Carolina
The earliest documented Vincent patriarch in the tree. Married Mary Anne Vincent Thomas on 5 May 1866 in Orange County, NC. Together they had 24 children — an extraordinary family that became the root system for every Vincent and Vinson in this archive. Their household in Leasburg and Caswell County, NC, is the ancestral home ground.
Generation 2 · ~1854
Marshal Vincent
Born: ~1854 · North Carolina · Died: 12 May 1934 · Caswell County, NC
Son of Greene Vincent. One of the 24. Married Ella Vincent in Caswell County (10 Jan 1891) — their children include Clarence L Vincent, who carries the line forward. Marshal and Ella are buried in the ancestral ground of Stony Creek, Caswell County. He lived to 80 — a long life that bridged Reconstruction and the Depression era.
Generation 3 · 1874
Clarence L Vincent
Born: 14 June 1874 · Caswell County, NC · Died: 6 November 1961 · Greensboro, NC
Son of Marshal + Ella Vincent. Married twice: first to Susie Smith Piles (25 Jul 1896, Caswell County — 17 children); after Susie's death in 1918, married Mamie Fannie Graves Wright (26 May 1920, Caswell County — 13 children). Clarence lived 87 years and fathered many children across both families. The Susie Smith Piles connection — a key ancestral line in Latisha's genealogical research. Clarence died in Greensboro, NC, completing the geographic shift from Caswell County to the Piedmont.
Generation 4 · 1934
Samuel Lee Vincent
Born: 30 July 1934 · Caswell County, NC · Died: 26 March 2026 · Greensboro, NC
Son of Clarence L Vincent + Mamie Fannie Graves Wright. The last of his generation. A beloved family historian who held the family's history in his memory when no one else could. Married Queen Esther Bigelow Vincent (23 Jun 1957, Guilford County) — she preceded him in death on 10 May 1984. Samuel lived 91 years. His death on 26 March 2026 was the closing of a direct living link to Caswell County, to the Great Migration generation, and to all that Clarence and Mamie built. He is central to the Ancestral Ground of this framework — one of the presences that continues in relationship.

Their family is rooted in Greensboro before the move south to the SC Lowcountry. The Walterboro and South Carolina connection begins with Christopher — the westward-to-coastal migration that places the family in Lowcountry territory.

The Vincent · Vinson Surname Split
The Same Root, Two Names

In the 1870 census, George Vincent appears in Greene Vincent's household in Caswell County, NC. In genealogical records, he also appears as George Brown VINSON — the same individual, two surnames. This is not an error. It is history.

After Emancipation, formerly enslaved people and free Black families faced the question of what to call themselves. Some took or retained the surname of the last enslaver. Some chose entirely new names. Some families split — some siblings taking one form, others another — the names diverging even as the blood remained the same.

The tree holds both: 77 Vincents and 9 Vinsons, all descending from Greene Vincent's household, all documented in Caswell County during the same Reconstruction-era years. The archive notes this explicitly: "VINSON — surname changed post-Emancipation; same ancestral line as VINCENT."

This is content. This is history. This is yours to tell.

The Origin Statement

Midnight Magnolia was not invented. It was remembered.

It came from a granddaughter who sat with a grandfather who held the family's history in his body when no one else could. From a Lowcountry porch and a Caswell County archive. From the moment she realized that the genealogy she was building and the healing work she was doing were the same practice — that tracing where you come from and understanding what you carry are not separate inquiries. They are one.

The brand is named for the liminal — midnight, the hour between worlds; magnolia, the flower that blooms where other things can't survive. It is a digital sanctuary for people who are healing in the dark: neurodivergent creators, people in recovery, people who are trying to figure out who they are without the noise of hustle culture telling them what they should want. People who, like the founder, are descended from people who survived things that were not supposed to be survivable.

The Lineage Paragraph

For About page, IAAM application, grant narrative, press bio. Adapt length as needed.

My grandfather Samuel Lee Vincent was the last of his generation — the youngest child of Clarence L Vincent and Mamie Fannie Graves Wright, whose family had been rooted in Caswell County, North Carolina since before Emancipation. He died on 26 March 2026 at 91 years old, and with him passed a living link to a Caswell County that no longer exists as it was — to the Stony Creek and Leasburg of the 1930s and 1940s, to the Great Migration years, to the years when the family still spelled its name both Vincent and Vinson depending on which census taker wrote it down.

He was the family historian. I am the archivist he was training me to be — though neither of us would have used that word. I have documented 939 members of our extended family, traced six generations from Greene Vincent born in 1820 to my son Noah Christopher Vincent born in 2011 in North Charleston, South Carolina, completing a geographic arc from the Piedmont to the Lowcountry that took 200 years. The names repeat themselves down the line — Christopher, Samuel, the initials preserved even when the full names changed. This is how families remember. This is what archives are made of.

The Framework Paragraph

For Theory of Change summaries, grant applications, MLIS application statements, brand pitches.

The Five Roots is a healing-centered theory of change I developed through Midnight Magnolia — not in a vacuum, but from lived experience, from the recovery frameworks that held me, from the clinical tools that gave language to what I already knew, and from a spiritual practice rooted in Black Southern ancestral tradition that I have never fully named in public and do not intend to. The framework has five pillars: naming what you feel, releasing what shamed you for feeling it, examining what you carry honestly, tending your life with cultivation rather than correction, and finding the community — living and ancestral — that will hold you while you do all of that. The Ancestral Ground beneath all five pillars is not a metaphor. It is epistemology. My grandfather is in there. Susie Smith Piles is in there. The 939 people whose names I have spent years learning to pronounce correctly are in there. Healing, in this framework, is always also intergenerational. The archive and the theory of change are the same document.

The IAAM Genealogical Writing Sample — Draft Seed
The Split — A Vincent / Vinson Surname Narrative

Seed paragraph for the IAAM writing sample. Expand from here.

In the 1870 census for Caswell County, North Carolina, the household of Greene Vincent is recorded: a free Black man born around 1820 in Person County, his wife Mary Anne, and their children — some bearing the name Vincent, one recorded as Vinson. This was not a clerical error. This was what freedom looked like in the first years after Emancipation: a family deciding, name by name, child by child, what they would carry forward and what they would set down.

The tree I have built holds both names: 77 Vincents and 9 Vinsons, all from the same Caswell County soil, all traceable to the same Greene and Mary Anne. The split in the name is also a record of choice — however constrained — made in the immediate aftermath of legal freedom. My surname is Vincent. My grandfather's was Vincent. His grandfather's was Vincent, though his grandfather's father may have been the one who decided which form of the name to keep and which to let drift into the next census column.

I am the archivist. I hold both names in the same spreadsheet. That is what reconciliation looks like when it happens at the level of the archive.

The archive and the theory of change are the same document.
